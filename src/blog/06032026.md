Often when asked about my research interests, I say that my research focuses on the intersection of Blackness, mathematics, and information. Recently, I have been thinking about the right configuration of these topics as I attempt to scope out a dissertation project. I have increasingly found that instead of one perfect intersection that brings these topics together, there are in fact many ways that Blackness, mathematics, and information interact that are worthy of investigation. Each of my projects as a PhD student and emerging researcher has been an attempt to understand and intervene in the complex set of relationships between blackness, mathematics, and information.   
Mathematics is often given an epistemological authority that appears above reproach due to its perceived objectivity. The utility of mathematics for technological advancement and understanding the world also greatly contributes to this epistemic unassailability. As physicist Eugene Wigner famously noted, mathematics seems to possess an *unreasonable effectiveness* that underpins countless technologies, engineering marvels, and scientific discoveries. For most people, math lies beneath the surface, and unless one is a specialist, there is no need to be concerned with what is going on as long as it works. However, the presumed objectivity of mathematics obscures the fact that mathematics is not a value-free system, but emerges from and is shaped by social and cultural dynamics.   
Mathematics Education researchers Powell and Frankenstein (1997) note, "Mathematics is a cultural product and, therefore, is created by humans in the interconnectedness of culture. The interactions are dialectical: people’s daily practice, language, and ideology affect and are affected by their mathematical knowledge" (p. 119). Martin (1997) argues that we can investigate mathematics as a product of society by investigating the social construction of mathematical knowledge and the social systems in which mathematics is created and used. These perspectives are indicative of the social constructionist and ethnomathematics paradigms. In contrast, other schools of thought such as platonism or mathematical realism hold that mathematical objects exist independently of human thought or experience. I am still reading through philosophy of mathematics literature and learning more about the various paradigms and schools of thought; however, in my work I emphasize the social character of mathematics. The tension between the Platonist claim of mathematical objectivity and the social constructionist perspective compels an interrogation into the practice of mathematics itself. My research agenda centers mathematics not as an eternal object but as a set of dynamic, historically contingent social practices and conceptual technologies that continue to shape and be shaped by socio-technical realities.   
Mathematics is often the least analyzed discipline of knowledge within academic knowledge production. It is taken for granted that mathematical knowledge and logical reasoning are fundamental to knowledge and thus abstract and objective. There has been work within STS and Critical Data Studies that seeks to critically analyze narratives of objectivity and abstraction by focusing on the history of data, the emergence of statistics and probability, and the social implications of statistical methods. For example, Hacking’s (1990) foundational text *The Taming of Chance* provides a comprehensive social history of the emergence of statistics and its connections to politics and culture. This text has been foundational for the development of critical data studies and understanding the role of statistics as an expression of biopower and the state. Similarly, Oscar Gandy Jr. (2010, 2016, 2021), across multiple writings, argues that statistical governance, despite claims of objectivity, plays a crucial role in the rationalization of discrimination. As a governance mechanism, statistics and probability extend surveillance to sort and order everyday life, reinforcing or exacerbating inequality in society. O’Neil (2016) analyzes how big data, algorithms, and mathematical methods reinforce racism and inequality. Amaro (2022) connects the emergence of the statistical and probabilistic method to racialized classification schemes and traces this legacy to modern-day computer vision systems.  
Because of this, I turn to infrastructure studies for approaches and examples of investigating technologies that are hidden yet provide the backbone of important aspects of society. Infrastructure Studies proceeds from the premise that infrastructure is often hidden. The goal of infrastructure studies is to make infrastructure visible by examining its social and material dimensions. Leigh Star (1999) describes infrastructure studies as such:  
“This article is in a way a call to study boring things. Many aspects of infrastructure are singularly unexciting. They appear as lists of numbers and technical specifications, or as hidden mechanisms subtending those processes more familiar to social scientists. It takes some digging to unearth the dramas inherent in system design, creating, to restore narrative to what appears to be dead lists.” (p. 377).   
Star’s explanation of infrastructure studies brings with it notions of illumination, perspective, and subjectivity that come up in the critical interrogation of infrastructure. When considering physical infrastructure, such as a power grid, we can understand the power lines in the ground as a form of infrastructure. It is hidden from view until something breaks and we are dramatically reminded of the fragile assemblage of materiality, governance, and social relations that enable the continued usage of such complex technical systems. Conceptualizing mathematics as infrastructure emphasizes how mathematics provides fundamental methods for the quantification, the formal representation of problems, and the search for optimal solutions. Yet for most users, developers, and casual observers, mathematics lies beneath the surface. As long as the methods work, they can be forgotten. However, as several scholars have observed, mathematics can obscure the very assumptions, norms, and values that can lead to negative social effects. In this way, mathematics functions as the infrastructure of Machine Learning, and it is necessary to illuminate and investigate this infrastructure.  
Within machine learning, mathematics provides methods of formalization and abstraction to turn real- world problems into machine learning problems. Formalization and abstraction are both social practices and cognitive technologies that allow for the extension of human capability. They are not in and of themselves problematic or harmful, but these practices are not value-free. As social practices, formalization and abstraction are political and often operations of power. Many scholars have documented the violence and harm of abstraction, particularly in the continued subjugation of Blackness in the wake of slavery (Hartman, 2008; McKittrick, 2014; Morgan, 2021; Rosenthal, 2018; Warren, 2019). As a core aspect of the dominant social scientific paradigm, quantification contributes to the furthering of racialization and the concretization of existing social reproduction and stratification (Dixon-Román, 2017). Quantification also has its limits. As da Silva (2022) argues:  
Statistics tell only half of the story, and, in doing so, they become part of the problem. How do you correlate the number of lashes a person’s ancestor received 190 years ago with their likelihood of being unemployed in July 2020? Or being in the criminal justice system? Or dying of COVID-19? (p. 166\)  
Centering mathematics attends us to this violence and historical imbrication of mathematics and power. However, mathematics is also a site of contestation and intervention. Methods, practices, norms, and the social relations these mathematical practices are embedded in are not immutable/static. We can do things differently by adopting new onto-epistemological groundings and developing frameworks that prioritize other values and logics (de Freitas et al., 2016; Johnson & Jabbari, 2022; Joque, 2022).  
Mathematics provides a powerful set of tools and modes of inquiry for designing and exploring formal systems (pure) and solving real-world problems (applied). Mathematics is often presented as a world of objective a priori truths, resting on a set of formal systems that have an unreasonable effectiveness in real-world applications. However, if we begin from the perspective that mathematics is fundamentally social, then we can interrogate practices, philosophies, and ideologies that shape and utilize mathematics. The history of mathematics, particularly the emergence of statistics, reveals how quantification has functioned as a mechanism for the rationalization of discrimination, reinforcing racial classification schemes and colonial logics.   
My purpose in exploring scholarship and writing on mathematics is to identify approaches, debates, and alternative systems that can inform my own research on the relationship between mathematics and society. I aim to move beyond critique and interrogation of mathematics to explore critical mathematical practice. I develop the term inspired by Agre’s term critical technical practice. Critical mathematical practice is a methodological approach that demands that we examine the assumptions of normative mathematical practice and the sociohistorical context of our mathematical methods. This critique is done alongside work that can be referred to as mathematical or quantitative while thinking reflectively/reflexively about the work, critiquing assumptions, and exploring other traditions and lineages outside of normative mathematical practice. Ultimately, in my work I  seek to use mathematics not merely as a site of inquiry, but also intervention and creativity to decolonize and reshape the hidden infrastructures of knowledge, quantification, and technology.   
This is a reading list I have put together which includes the many books, articles, and writings I have encountered as I seek to develop my approach to *critical mathematical practice*. My critical engagement with mathematics is specifically concerned with thinking Blackness and Mathematics together. This list includes work by scholars including critical theory, historical analysis, philosophical perspectives, and critical methodologies for quantitative practice.
